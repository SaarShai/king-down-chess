Round 2 — your three references

Your three screenshots, each with three builds of the engine aimed at it. Same position everywhere; the live links open the real game with that preset.

Voxel options from above (A1, A3, B2) suffer from the sculpts' round base discs; the iso camera (C1, C3) shows the sculpts in profile.

A — Chunky outlined tactics

  • Big pixels, saturated flat colour, almost no gradients.
  • A 1-pixel near-black outline on everything — units, props, and the tile edges.
  • Terrain as stacked blocks: lighter top, darker side, no texture.
  • Even light, no cast shadows; state shown as overlays.

A1Chunky voxel

Pixel 3 with two toon bands, a 1-art-pixel hull rim and a dark ring around every tile top; the flattest, most graphic option, but two bands throw away most of the sculpts’ modelling.

Try it live → ?style=chunkyVoxel

A2Chunky sprite

Painted sprites with a dilated near-black outline over the same ringed tiles; the crispest silhouettes of the nine, at the cost of the pieces never turning with the camera.

Try it live → ?style=chunkySprite

A3Chunky + Endesga 32

Chunky voxel quantised to the Endesga-32 ramp for saturated poster colour; the strongest graphic unity, but the ramp swallows the tile edge ring on dark squares and pulls both armies toward the same navy.

Try it live → ?style=chunkyPalette

B — Painterly dungeon

  • Small pixels, painterly shading, five or more tones per material.
  • No outlines — figures separate by value contrast and a soft shadow.
  • Dark ambient with warm torch lights and a vignette.
  • Textured stone tiles on a visible grid; camera high (~60°) and straight on.

B1Dungeon sprite

Painted sprites on Drive stone tiles under four warm corner torches at 60°, with contact shadows; the most atmospheric, but the far corners go dark enough to hurt piece identification.

Try it live → ?style=dungeonSprite

B2Dungeon voxel

Same stone board and torches with voxel sculpts, so the pieces catch the warm light in 3D; the light falloff costs contrast on the dark army more than the sprites do.

Try it live → ?style=dungeonVoxel

B3Dungeon bright

Stone tiles and contact shadows under the existing bright paper lighting; keeps reference B’s textured floor and full readability, but loses the torch mood entirely.

Try it live → ?style=dungeonBright

C — Iso pixel

  • True isometric: 45° azimuth, about 30° elevation, orthographic.
  • Warm 32-colour palette, dithered ground, outline only on silhouettes.
  • Terrain as chunky blocks with visible side faces.
  • Small figures, roughly 32–40 art pixels tall.

C1Iso voxel

True 45°/30° isometric on a tall board block with visible side faces, warm 32-colour ramp and a hull rim; the strongest sense of place, but a 30° camera makes front pieces occlude the rank behind.

Try it live → ?style=isoVoxel

C2Iso sprite

The iso board with outlined painted sprites on the warm ramp; the closest match to the reference art, though billboards at 30° stand oddly tall against the flat board.

Try it live → ?style=isoSprite

C3Iso clean

Iso camera and tall board with no palette quantisation, so the painted voxel colours survive; clearer piece colour than C1, but it loses the limited-palette cohesion the reference lives on.

Try it live → ?style=isoClean

A — Rendering approaches

Five presets already in the build. Switch live with ?style=.

A1HD-2D sprites  ?style=sprites

Each piece is one pre-rendered billboard cut straight from the miniature PSD render: trimmed, box-downscaled to 128 px, given a 1 px ink outline, and leaned back to match the camera. This is the Donkey Kong Country trick — sculpt once, render once, ship flat art — and the same idea Octopath Traveler revived as HD-2D.

Clarity: best of the five. All eleven types separate at a glance — the archer's bow, the paladin's shield, the beast's jaw, the maester's hat. The warm/cool split names the side before you read any shape. The only cost is the tall back-rank pieces leaning over the pawns in front.

Cost: near zero. The art exists; one ImageMagick pass per army, about a second for 22 sprites. Locked to one camera angle.

A2HD pixel  ?style=hd

Real 3-D voxel pieces, flat-lit, with the whole frame snapped to a 2 px grid and an outline pass driven by normals and depth. It is the low-resolution 3-D of Fez with the ink line of Crossy Road on top.

Clarity: poor at piece level. Side is obvious (navy against bone) but identity is not — archer, maester and paladin land as the same lump, and only the king and queen separate by height. The white army also washes out against the pale squares.

Cost: high. Auto-voxelising the sculpts is what produced these lumps; a readable set means hand-building each piece in Goxel, roughly 1–3 h per piece, 1–2 weeks for twelve.

A3Cel pixel  ?style=cel

The same voxels with toon shading: light quantised into four flat bands, plus a stronger depth edge. The look sits next to The Legend of Zelda: The Wind Waker or Jet Set Radio, scaled down to pixels.

Clarity: slightly worse than A2. The edges are cleaner, but banding throws away the shading that was doing the little work of telling the shapes apart — the white army is now nearly flat white.

Cost: same asset bill as A2. The toon material itself is free.

A4Clean voxel  ?style=voxel

The voxels again, but no pixel snap (1 px) and depth outlines only — the crisp, un-pixelated flat-shaded look of Crossy Road.

Clarity: sharpest of the voxel three, and still not enough. The geometry is clean, so the silhouette problem is now impossible to blame on the pixel filter. It also stops reading as pixel art and starts reading as low-poly 3-D, which is a different game.

Cost: same asset bill as A2, and it drops the pixel-art identity the UI is built around.

A516-bit palette  ?style=db32

The finished frame quantised to DB32, a 32-colour palette, with ordered dithering — the constraint that gave the SNES and Mega Drive era its look.

Clarity: worst here. Dither noise eats the white army, the board flips to slate and white, and the pieces speckle into blue-orange confetti. The palette is fighting the assets rather than styling them.

Cost: the shader is free; making it look deliberate means a palette authored for these pieces, and it can only ever sit on top of a voxel set that already reads.

B — Sprite treatments

A1 held fixed; one knob moved at a time. ?army=, ?px=, ?spriteScale=.

B1Army: green / purple

Reads as well as blue/red, but green sits close to the board's tan and gives up a little separation on the light squares.

B2Army: ivory / charcoal

Graded from the uncoloured base renders. The closest match to the paper-and-ink UI; charcoal keeps a crisp silhouette but loses most interior detail.

B3Pixel size 1

No pixel snap. Maximum detail — faces, spear tips, the shield rim — but it reads as a printed board game, not as pixel art.

B4Pixel size 3

Chunky and the most honest as pixel art; spears, faces and the archer's bow start to break up into single stray pixels.

B5Sprite scale 0.85

Pieces sit inside their own squares and the back rank stops leaning over the pawns. The safest setting for the phone layout.

B6Sprite scale 1.15

More presence and more drama, at the price of a back rank that crowds the pawns and tall pieces that clear the board frame.

C — Mobile

390×844, the same position. The rail becomes a bottom sheet and the board keeps the top 58 % of the screen.

M1Sprites

Pieces survive the shrink: the beast, the paladin and the queen are still distinct at thumb size.

M2HD pixel

The voxel pieces collapse into one repeated blob. This is the clearest argument in the whole board.

D — Sprite sheets

The baked art itself, straight from tools/sprites.sh.

128 px with outline — what ships today

Full bake height. One 1 px #14121f outline pulled from the dilated alpha, which is what keeps a piece off the board texture.

96 px with outline — phone-sized

Roughly the pixels a phone actually shows. Detail holds; the outline starts doing most of the work.

All four army colours

Blue, red, green and purple from the original PSDs. Ivory and charcoal are graded from the uncoloured renders instead, so any two of the six can be paired.
