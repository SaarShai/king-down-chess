Game Design

Summary
        The kingdom is at war. The capital is being attacked from the North (the mountains, the “Highlands”), the South (the forests, the “___”), the East (the icebergs, the “___”) and the West (the mines, the “Firelands”). The Good King must protect the city.


        You are a lowly pike who got left behind…
Goal
The goal of “Episode 1” is to return to the capital by completing a set number of levels which span throughout the forest lands in the South.
Future episodes will have both the forest and the plot thicken and lead you, the player, to explore the other lands and the capital itself, opening up new levels and goals.


Gameplay
        King Down is an open-ended turn-based strategy game with RPG elements. The player controls a “hero” character which progresses through levels, each presenting a different strategic challenge. By completing levels, the hero becomes more powerful, acquires new items and recruits new party members, all of which assists with completing next levels.
        It is a story-driven game that is built like a TV show, where new episodes are constantly released. These episodes reveal more of the story, progressing the journey of the characters, and introduce new challenges. Players are going to tune in not only for the gameplay experience but also to uncover the plot’s twists and turns. As much attention and resources are going to be given to developing the story as are going to be invested in expanding the playable offerings.

Level Mechanics
        Each level is a matrix of tiles that enclose an area where the hero and his party members (all units, occupying a single tile) must complete an objective (and optionally sub-goals). There are also meta-game challenges that reward players with trophies for accomplishing them while playing the level (and possibly independently from the main objective and sub-goals).
        Areas are defined by a unique arrangements and types of the tiles, a graphic style, a story behind the location and/or event and enemies and environments. For example, a fiery castle ruled by an evil witch, which has 4 rooms, each with 8x8 tiles, 4 columns that block arrows, and 3 “beast” units spread randomly inside.


        Once a level is “launched”, the player is presented his hero and party members, ready to explore the area (sometimes sections of the area are under “fog of war”). The starting status of the party depends on customization that can be made in the profile section of the game, where the player can assign items, spells, units, etc. as the starting status for the next level “launched”. Different levels require (or are easier to complete with) different starting statuses, and it’s up to the player to decide the best status, according to previous attempts at accomplishing the level’s goal. Challenges also exist in order to “recycle” completed levels to present a different experience (and win strategy) every time you replay them.


        During the levels, players can move their party members along the tiles, depending on the movement profile of each member. They can also engage in combat with enemy units positioned on occupied tiles. Players can also “cast” spells to influence the party members (boost), enemy units (cripple), environment (modify) and conditions (set) from a book of spells which can be customized and upgraded.


        Levels progress in turns, alternating between the player and any number of enemy parties (computer controlled). During a turn, the player has a limited number of actions they can perform, yet they may pass on any remaining actions if they so choose.


        Once a player achieves the goal of the level, a review is presented (stylized by a narrated (text or voice) summary), rewards are bestowed, and the player returns to the overworld in order to continue their quest to the next level.
